# Charlie Munger’s Almanack

Use the [Stripe Press digital edition](https://stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack). Distinguish Munger’s argument from a contributor’s recollection, the editor’s commentary, and your own application. Use the [source guide](references/source-guide.md) to find the relevant talk or chapter. For a question about the book, answer it directly, identify the speaker and setting, and link the passage’s section. Verify exact wording in the reader before quoting. Use short quotations sparingly.

## Work a decision

1. **Frame the choice.** Name the decision-maker, the alternative they give up, and what failure would cost them. Include waiting if it is feasible. Locate the edge of competence. Which part can they judge from experience or evidence? Which uncertainty could change the choice?
2. **Work forward.** Pick the few models from different disciplines that explain this case. For each, connect an observed fact to a mechanism and an observable consequence. Give the important quantities units. Rough arithmetic can eliminate an appealing story before detailed modeling begins.
3. **Work backward.** Imagine a plausible route to a bad outcome. Identify what makes it possible, whether the loss can be survived, and a fact or change in design that would interrupt it. Seek the strongest evidence for the opposing case.
4. **Check the people, including the analyst.** Trace who gets paid, praised, penalized, or heard when each option wins. Two apparently independent endorsements may come from one incentive. Where several pressures reinforce each other, use the [misjudgment guide](references/misjudgment.md) to find a countermeasure.
5. **Make the call.** Give the judgment, its decisive assumption, and the evidence that would reverse it. When the missing fact is obtainable, name the cheapest informative test. A decision that belongs in the “too hard” pile deserves a clear reason. Record the initial reasoning if the outcome can later be reviewed.

For a business or security, use the [investment guide](references/investment.md) to connect customer economics, owner cash, price and the next-best use of capital. For an institutional or personal decision, stay with the user’s own objective and unit of success. Match the depth of the answer to the size and reversibility of the decision.

Historical investments and anecdotes illustrate reasoning. Verify present-day facts independently and date material inputs. Treat a bias as a testable explanation of behavior. Give a competing explanation when the evidence is ambiguous. The worked examples and decision procedures here are original applications of the book.
