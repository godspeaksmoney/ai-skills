# From a suspected bias to a better decision

Munger assembled 25 tendencies from reading and experience. The final item concerns the outsized effects of tendencies operating together. His discussion ranges from useful habits to costly mistakes. Use the setting to select a plausible mechanism, then look for evidence that distinguishes it from an ordinary explanation. Munger acknowledges the limits of his account in the closing questions. [Talk Eleven](https://stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-eleven)

| When this matters | How to investigate | Change to try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An adviser benefits when a transaction closes | Compare the advice with what someone paid for the subsequent result would recommend. Include unconscious selection of favorable evidence. | Pay for durable results where measurable. Have a disinterested reviewer evaluate the assumptions. |
| Several respected people agree quickly | Trace where each person got their information. A senior person's endorsement can produce a roomful of apparent witnesses. | Collect private estimates and reasons before revealing the sponsor’s view. |
| A team defends an earlier public commitment | Check the estimate it made before committing, what has changed, and which facts would have mattered to a newcomer. | Allow views to change before the final vote. Compare alternatives from today forward. |
| Losing something already possessed dominates the debate | Identify what the decision-maker experiences as theirs. Test whether they would acquire it today at the same terms. | Separate transition costs and genuine customer disruption from attachment to the status quo. |
| A demonstration, favor, familiar brand or likeable seller attracts approval | Identify the actual performance claim. Ask whether gratitude, association, liking or a vivid demonstration could deliver the same response. | Run a comparable trial on ordinary cases with the evaluator blind to the supplier when practical. |
| A recent vivid loss or an attractive headline price dominates | Recover the full set of comparable outcomes and a meaningful price baseline. Changing the presentation can change the judgment with the underlying facts held constant. | Compare absolute costs and frequencies on the same scale. |
| Stress, urgency, or unfamiliar work degrades performance | Check whether the conditions for clear judgment or a rehearsed response still exist. A short deadline may have a real cost as well. | Rehearse a rare high-consequence event, allow a pause when feasible, and make it easy to report bad news. |

## Work the interaction

**Fictional example.** A supplier offers a one-day discount. The procurement lead has publicly endorsed it. Three managers agree after hearing the lead speak. The deadline makes delay feel like a loss, the endorsement makes reversal embarrassing, and the speaking order suppresses independent estimates. Three votes now contain much less new information than they appear to.

Obtain the total cost of the best available substitute, ask the managers privately what failure would look like, and request evidence that the deadline reflects a genuine capacity constraint. A documented shortage plus three independently collected usage estimates would strengthen the case for buying now. A recurring “last chance” quote would weaken it. Assigning a numerical probability to each bias would add precision without evidence.

Use the same reasoning to improve the decision-maker’s own procedure. Make sound conduct easier, preserve others’ ability to choose, and welcome information that could overturn the initial story.
