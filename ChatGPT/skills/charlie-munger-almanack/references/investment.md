# From a good business to a decision at a price

## Who keeps the benefit?

Follow one unit from customer payment to cash available to owners. What makes customers buy again? Can a competitor copy the advantage or bid it away? When equipment lowers cost, compare industry capacity, contract resets and pricing behavior. In a competitive industry the customer may capture the gain. A firm with durable distribution or pricing power may keep some of it. Identify a piece of evidence that distinguishes these cases. ## What does the buyer receive?

Start from current dated filings and a dated price in the same currency and share units. Reconcile reported profit to sustainable owner cash using appropriate maintenance investment, working capital, taxes and financing obligations. Test how grants, dilution and repurchases affect the existing owner’s claim. Count a compensation or dilution cost once and explain its treatment. For a bank or insurer, consider distributable capital after regulatory or underwriting needs. Compare the price with a defensible range using explicit assumptions. Match enterprise value with cash flows to the business, or equity value with cash flows to equity. Report per-share values on a consistent share count. Contrast the prospective return with an available alternative, on the same horizon and after material costs. Show what has to go right at the quoted price and how permanent loss could occur. If valuation depends on an unknowable input, explain why the decision is too hard at this price. A watchlist should say what would turn waiting into action. Specify the price or evidence that would make the payoff attractive and the funding terms the owner could live with. Concentration requires an unusual opportunity, genuine understanding and the ability to hold through losses. Munger’s own holdings describe his circumstances. ## Worked example: a factory upgrade

A fictional price-taking supplier can spend 12 million today to cut annual operating costs by 4 million for five years. Its contracts reset yearly and competitors can buy the same machine. A simple screen shows why the machine’s advertised savings cannot settle the decision.

| Share of savings the supplier retains | Annual benefit | Five-year present value at 10% | Value after the 12 million outlay |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 100% | 4.0m | 15.16m | +3.16m |
| 50% | 2.0m | 7.58m | −4.42m |

These illustrative year-end flows ignore taxes, residual value and implementation costs. Break-even retention is roughly 79%. If customers capture half the savings after each reset, funding the equipment on its stated savings alone destroys value. If refusing to upgrade would lose existing contracts, estimate the contribution those contracts would otherwise earn and the cost of exiting the line. The best next evidence is a renewal quote against a rival that already owns the machine. Apply the same comparison to buying the supplier’s shares once a price and a sustainable owner-cash estimate are available. [Talk Two](https://stripe.press/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-two)

## Whose capital and on what terms?

A good long-run payoff can still force a sale if debt, redemptions or spending obligations fall due first. Match leverage, liquidity and the time horizon to the owner. For an endowment or similar pool, show what remains in real terms after all-in costs and distributions. Compare an active mandate with a low-cost feasible alternative and identify the repeatable advantage needed to pay for the difference. Distinguish an apparent gain in quoted wealth from cash the institution can spend through a downturn.
