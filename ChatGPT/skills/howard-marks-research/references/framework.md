# Decisions drawn from the memos

The period notes preserve individual arguments and historical calls. Use this guide to find the decisions that recur across them. The questions and applications are this project’s synthesis.

## 1. Decide what a superior result would look like

Consistently avoiding disasters can compound into a remarkable long-term record even when annual rankings rarely impress. The distinction matters most for lending, where one large loss can overwhelm many coupons. Equity can have the opposite payoff shape: a handful of unusually large winners may carry the portfolio. For a stock with a long runway, investigate what would justify continued ownership after a price rise. For a lender to the same firm, identify the asset coverage and priority of the claim. (2023)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/fewer-losers-or-more-winners) [Selling Out (2022)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/selling-out)

## 2. Find the expectation embedded in the price

First establish who gets paid, how much and when. Then work backward from the quote. At the required return, what unit economics, reinvestment period, pricing power and exit valuation would support it? For a convertible a buyer can overpay for a promising pairing of equity upside and a bond floor when fund flows crowd the trade. For an equity, profitable reinvestment and intangible assets can make book value a poor anchor. A view that differs from consensus earns its keep only when there is evidence for that difference and enough potential gain to cover mistakes. ## 3. Explain who is willing or obliged to take the other side

Institutional restrictions, stigma, benchmark mandates, forced sales and expensive specialist research can sustain a mispricing. Name the mechanism and the reason the investor researching it can bear the cost. A low multiple can also arise from informed expectations of lower earnings. Write both explanations down. A rally or decline alone cannot adjudicate them. Check whether an investor who saw the same change in fundamentals could already have traded on it. [How Does an Inefficient Market Get That Way? ## 4. Put permanent loss on a timeline

Identify the underlying event, the contract or cash-flow channel through which it reaches the security holder, and the point at which recovery becomes impossible. Leverage can convert temporary marks into irreversible sales. A callable loan, collateral deadline or redemption promise can bind before collateral recovers. Show available unencumbered resources, the timing of claims, and who can force a transaction. A manager’s ability to wait depends as much on the structure of funding as on the quality of analysis. ## 5. Read the cycle through the terms people accept

The lending cycle is visible in contract terms before defaults appear. More bidders and money to deploy can give borrowers the power to demand looser covenants, greater leverage and lower spreads. A good economy can coexist with a poor vintage of investments. A credit shutdown can later create bargains when forced sellers need cash. Check who must transact, who can wait and whether the price adequately compensates the buyer even if the business worsens. Recalibrate exposure in stages when the evidence changes. ## 6. Make uncertainty visible in the decision

Record plausible paths, evidence favoring each and which assumption has the largest effect. Stacking optimistic assumptions about demand, competitive position and financing can produce a fragile valuation even when each assumption seems reasonable in isolation. Assign event probabilities only where the evidence supports them. Explain any threshold at which the decision would change. Also ask what is lost by staying on the sidelines if the downside never materializes. [The Value of Predictions, or Where'd All This Rain Come From? ## 7. Separate the economics of an asset from the claim on it

A bond’s return is largely bounded by contract and by the price paid. Model defaults, recovery, fees, leverage and reinvestment before comparing a stated yield with an equity return. An equity owner can participate in unexpected success and bears the residual loss. Liquid and private forms of exposure can finance the same debtor. Smooth reporting or restrictions on withdrawals tell little about ultimate credit losses. Compare the actual owner payouts, in the same currency and over the same period, with a feasible alternative. ## 8. Trace incentives through regulation and technological change

Start with what the intervention changes for each participant. A price cap, tariff or new platform rule may shift costs or prompt suppliers to withdraw. Follow second-round responses before converting a public benefit into a shareholder benefit. Likewise, technological progress can benefit customers while competition and capital spending absorb most of the producers’ gains. Identify the layer of the industry with a defensible claim on cash flows, and who bears the financing risk if adoption falls short. ## 9. Evaluate a record in the circumstances that produced it

Start with the original mandate, risks taken and market conditions. A defensive manager can lag in a strong market and fulfill the mandate. To distinguish skill and luck, examine the decision available at the time, the other outcomes that could have occurred, leverage and benchmark exposure. For a fund, reconcile commitments, capital actually deployed, holding time, fees and distributions. A spectacular reported IRR on briefly deployed capital may translate into only modest wealth for the person who made the commitment. [What's It All About, Alpha? ## 10. Size for both opportunity and staying power

Distinguish the risk a person or institution *wants* from what its liabilities allow it to bear. Investments with different names can share a borrower, a funding channel or the same economic assumption. Evaluate those common exposures under stress. For an individual company assessment, explain the risk of missing a substantial upside and the conditions that favor waiting. A personalized position size requires the owner’s liquidity needs, obligations and other holdings. [What’s Your Game Plan? (2003)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2003-09-05-whats-your-game-plan.pdf?sfvrsn=7bbc0f65_6) [Dare to Be Great (2006)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2006-09-07-dare-to-be-great.pdf?sfvrsn=b3bc0f65_6) [Liquidity (2015)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/2015-03-25-liquidity.pdf?sfvrsn=2dc70f65_6) [Calibrating (2020)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/docs/default-source/memos/calibrating.pdf?sfvrsn=3e98b665_6) [A Look Under the Hood (2025)](https://www.oaktreecapital.com/insights/memo/a-look-under-the-hood)
